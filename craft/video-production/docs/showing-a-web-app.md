# Showing a web app on screen

Most launch and feature videos for software are, at heart, a web or mobile app on screen. Getting that right is its own craft: a raw screen recording is not a product video, and a good app shown badly — tiny in a dead frame, silent, with an aimless cursor — sinks an otherwise strong script. This page is how to portray an app on screen, tool-agnostic like the rest of this reference.

## The subject decides the frame

Choose how much device context to show — it is a decision, not a default:

- **Bare viewport.** The app's own canvas, no chrome. Cleanest and most common for a focused feature moment; the UI fills the frame.
- **Browser chrome.** The viewport inside a browser window (address bar, tabs). Use it when "it's a web app, right in your browser" is part of the message, or an on-screen URL/address is the proof.
- **Device shell.** The UI inside a laptop, phone, or tablet frame. Use it to say *where* the app lives — a phone shell for a mobile app, a laptop for a desktop web app — or to place two contexts side by side (the same app on a phone and a laptop). A device shell is scene-setting; drop it once you are actually demonstrating, so the bezel does not steal frame from the UI.

Orientation and aspect follow the subject: a desktop web app is a landscape (16:9) frame; a mobile app is a portrait (9:16) frame in a phone shell. Don't force a mobile app into a wide frame, or a desktop app into a tall one.

## Real, rebuilt, or stylized — never fake

An app can appear at three levels of fidelity; pick one consciously:

- **Real capture** — the actual running product. The default for B2B: buyers discount what they cannot see. Show at least one real workflow in the real interface.
- **Faithful rebuild** — a clean recreation of the real screens, for a confidential, pre-release, or too-dense-to-read UI. It must match the product's real layout and language.
- **Clearly stylized** — a simplified, obviously illustrative version (enlarged type, a few elements, schematic). Good for a fast conceptual beat.

The one hard rule: **never a fake that reads as real.** A sloppy mock that looks like a screenshot but isn't is the worst option — it reads as deceptive, and as AI slop. Real, or honestly stylized; nothing in the uncanny middle. This is the same bar as the capability-proof rule in [anatomy-of-a-launch-video.md](anatomy-of-a-launch-video.md).

**And never noisy.** A real capture arrives carrying the live site's ephemeral UI — an exit-intent popup, a chat or mascot launcher in the corner, a cookie bar, a sticky promo. None of it belongs in the frame; it reads as unpolished and pulls the eye off the message. Strip it before the capture is used, and keep only what the beat is about. The one thing to protect while stripping is the hero itself — clean the chrome, not the content.

## Direct the eye — one focus at a time

A full app screen is busy; the viewer looks where you point. The toolkit, strongest first:

- **Scale to it.** Push the camera in / zoom so the element the voiceover names fills the frame. This is the primary move — most "where do I look" problems are solved by framing, not by decoration.
- **Ring or spotlight.** A highlight around the element, or dimming everything else, when the element must stay in context (you can't crop away the surrounding UI).
- **Labeled callout.** A short label naming the thing — a few words, not a sentence.
- **Cursor presence.** The pointer itself draws the eye to where the action is.

One focus per beat, and it lands **on the word that names it** — the ring appears, the camera settles, as the voiceover says the thing. A highlight that fires early or late reads as broken.

## Interaction is the motion

An app video should move because the app is being *used*, not because a static screenshot is slowly zoomed. The real interactions, each anchored to the spoken word that triggers it:

- **Click / tap** — the cursor travels to the target, presses, and ripples; or a tap blooms on touch. Reveal or cut on the press.
- **Type** — text appears in a field as if typed, with the result often held just offscreen for the payoff.
- **Pan / scroll** — move across or down a screen too big for one frame.
- **Zoom** — push in to a detail, pull out to restore context.
- **Swipe / drag** — the mobile gestures: a card swiped away, a sheet dragged up, a carousel moved.
- **State swap** — before/after, empty→full, toggle off→on — shown as two states cut on the beat, not morphed within one frame.

Motion must mean something: a real click, a real reveal, a real state change — never an aimless drift over a dead screen.

## The sound of the interface

Picture is only half of it. A video that is visually sharp but silent except for the voiceover reads as a screen-grab; the interface should be *heard*. These are **diegetic** sounds — they come from the UI itself, distinct from the music bed and the voiceover:

- **Click / tap** on a press.
- **Keystroke** on typing.
- **Whoosh / swish** on a transition, a scroll, a panel opening, a swipe.
- **Pop / chime** on a success, a completion, a result landing.

Rules: a few **deliberate** sounds on the key beats, not a sound on every pixel — over-sounding is as amateur as silence. Each sound lands **with its motion** (the click on the press frame, the whoosh as the panel moves). Diegetic UI sound sits under the voiceover and over the bed; it never fights the voice. This layer is the single most-skipped thing that separates a produced product video from a muted capture.

## Checklist

- Device context (bare / browser / device shell) chosen on purpose; dropped once demonstrating.
- Orientation and aspect follow the app (desktop landscape, mobile portrait).
- UI is real, a faithful rebuild, or clearly stylized — never a fake that reads real.
- Frame is clean — no popup, chat/mascot launcher, cookie bar, or sticky promo in shot; hero intact.
- At least one real workflow shown (B2B).
- One focus per beat, landed on the naming word; scale first, decorate second.
- The app is *used* — real clicks/types/scrolls/swipes/state-changes, not a drift over a still.
- Key interactions are heard — a few deliberate, diegetic UI sounds, each synced to its motion.
